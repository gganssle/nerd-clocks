How to read it

The big tubes show Unix time: the number of seconds since midnight UTC on 1 January 1970, the moment computers everywhere count from. The lamps below show the same number in 32-bit binary, the leftmost lamp being the sign bit, and the cyan display shows it in hexadecimal. Your local time is on the small tubes.

The Year 2038 problem

For decades Unix systems stored time in a time_t that was a signed 32-bit integer. Its largest value is 231 − 1 = 2,147,483,647, which is 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038. One second later the sign bit flips, the number wraps to −2,147,483,648, and a naive clock jumps back to December 1901.

0x7FFFFFFF + 1 → 0x80000000 → 1901-12-13 20:45:52

Modern 64-bit systems moved on long ago; a 64-bit time_t lasts about 292 billion years. The risk lives on in embedded devices, file formats and database columns that still pack time into 32 bits. Watch the sign lamp: when it lights, that is the moment.

Things Unix time ignores

Unix time pretends every day has exactly 86,400 seconds. The 27 leap seconds added since 1972 simply are not counted: during a leap second the timestamp repeats or stalls. That keeps date math simple but means Unix time is not a true count of elapsed SI seconds.

How a Nixie tube works

Each tube holds ten wire cathodes shaped like digits, stacked one behind another, inside a glass envelope of low-pressure neon. A honeycomb mesh anode sits in front. Put about 170 V across the anode and one cathode, and the gas ionizes into a cold-cathode glow discharge that wraps that digit in orange light. The unlit digits stay visible behind it as faint wires, which is why each number sits at a slightly different depth.

Nerd note

Nixies were made from the mid-1950s until LEDs replaced them in the 1970s. The name probably comes from a Burroughs draftsman's label "NIX I", for "Numeric Indicator eXperimental No. 1". The next round-number moment is shown under the countdown: 1,800,000,000 arrives on 15 January 2027.
