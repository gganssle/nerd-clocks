How to read it

The amber planet is the hour hand; read it against the amber numerals 1 to 12 on the big orbit. The blue planet is the minute hand, read against the minute marks 00 to 55 on the inner orbit. The small violet moon circling the blue planet completes one lap per minute and marks the seconds. Every body passes periapsis, its closest point to the star, at the top of its cycle: 12 o'clock, :00 minutes, :00 seconds.

The dial marks are not evenly spaced. They crowd together far from the star and spread out near it, because that is where the planets really slow down and speed up.

Kepler's three laws (1609–1619)

  1. Orbits are ellipses with the star at one focus, not the centre. The other focus is empty; it is marked with a small cross.
  2. Equal areas in equal times. The line from star to planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals. Each coloured wedge here covers one hour (or five minutes), and every wedge has the same area: short and fat near the star, long and thin far away.
  3. T² ∝ a³. The square of the period grows as the cube of the orbit's size. A planet four times farther out takes eight times longer to go around.

How it's computed

Time gives the mean anomaly M, an angle that grows perfectly steadily. Kepler's equation turns it into the eccentric anomaly E, solved each frame with a few Newton iterations, and E gives the position on the ellipse:

M = 2π · t / T M = E − e · sin E x = a (cos E − e), y = a √(1 − e²) sin E

There is no closed-form solution for E. Kepler himself solved it by trial and error, and the problem has been a favourite testbed for numerical methods ever since.

Real eccentricities

These orbits are stretched for drama (e = 0.40, 0.30, 0.25). Earth's orbit has e = 0.017, practically a circle; even so, we are 5 million km closer to the Sun in January than in July. Mercury has e = 0.206, Pluto 0.249, and Halley's Comet 0.967: it moves about 60 times faster at perihelion than at aphelion.
