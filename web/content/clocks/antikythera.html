How to read it

Three pointers share the centre of the open zodiac ring, as on a normal clock: the slim orange needle is seconds, the pale needle is minutes, and the wide pointer carrying the little sun is the hour. The Greek numerals Α Β Γ Δ Ε Ϛ Ζ Η Θ Ι ΙΑ ΙΒ are 1 to 12. The fourth pointer, with the black-and-silver ball on its tip, is the Moon; the ball shows the phase, and it comes back round to the sun at each new moon.

How it works

Everything you can see turning is a gear ratio, not an animation curve. A wheel meshing another divides the speed by the ratio of their tooth counts and reverses it:

ω₂ = −ω₁ · N₁ / N₂

The motion work steps a wheel that turns once a minute down through 16:80, 15:60 and 20:60 to the minute wheel, then through 12:48 and 12:36 to the twelve-hour wheel at the centre — 5 × 4 × 3 × 4 × 3 = 720 minutes per turn.

Hanging off that same central shaft is the lunar train of the real mechanism, with its original tooth counts:

64/38 × 48/24 × 127/32 = 254/19 = 13.368…

254 sidereal months in 19 years is one of the numbers the Greeks actually built into the brass — and 254 − 19 = 235 synodic months, the Metonic cycle, which is why the moon phase ball works out for free from the angle between the two pointers.

The back dials

  • Metonic: a five-turn spiral divided into 235 lunar months. 19 solar years and 235 lunar months agree to within two hours, which is how ancient calendars kept months and seasons in step.
  • Saros: a four-turn spiral of 223 months (18.03 years), the eclipse repeat cycle. If an eclipse happens today, another very like it happens one Saros later, shifted 120° west because 223 months is 18 years and a third of a day.

Nerd note

The real device, hauled out of a shipwreck off Antikythera in 1901, had about 30 surviving bronze gears cut by hand, including a 223-tooth wheel and a pin-and-slot pair that reproduces the Moon's varying speed around its elliptical orbit. Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known for the next 1,400 years. The one liberty taken here is the timescale: one turn of the central wheel is twelve hours instead of a year, so the lunar train runs fast enough to watch.
