How to read it

Midnight starts in the bottom-left corner. The glowing head crawls along a single continuous line that visits all 65,536 cells of a 256 × 256 grid exactly once, reaching the bottom-right corner at the next midnight. Each cell is 1.318 seconds. The lit part is the day so far. Each hour has its own colour and fills its own patch, labelled with the hour. The exact time is on the right.

How it works

This is an order-8 Hilbert curve, described by David Hilbert in 1891 a year after Peano found the first space-filling curve. It is built recursively: split the square into four quadrants, visit them in a U-shape, and inside each quadrant draw a smaller, rotated copy of the same U. Eight levels of that recursion give 48 cells.

Because of the recursion, a cell's position is just its index written in base 4: every digit picks one of four quadrants, one zoom level at a time. The dashed nested boxes around the head show the first four digits of the current base-4 address.

first quadrant = 00:00–06:00 second quadrant = 06:00–12:00 (and so on)

Why it matters

  • Locality: moments that are close in time are always close on the square. Compare the unrolled timeline bar to the square: every hour stays a compact blob, never a stripe.
  • Databases and map services use Hilbert ordering to store nearby places on nearby disk pages (e.g. spatial indexes, Google's S2 geometry library).
  • It has been used to lay out the IPv4 address space: xkcd's 2006 "Map of the Internet" puts neighbouring address blocks next to each other this way.

Nerd note

As the order goes to infinity the curve passes through every point of the square. A one-dimensional line ends up completely filling a two-dimensional area, which is part of why "dimension" is a subtler idea than it looks.
