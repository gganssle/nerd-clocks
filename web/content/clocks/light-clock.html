How to read it

On the left is a clock at rest: two mirrors one light-second apart, and a photon bouncing between them. Up and back is one second — that is the whole mechanism. On the right is the identical clock flying past at β, which the minute sets: nearly at rest on the hour, 0.99 c by :59. Its photon has to travel the dashed zig-zag, because the mirrors move while the light is in flight. Same speed, longer path, fewer ticks. The line at the bottom tells you how many seconds the flying clock loses over the current minute.

How it works

Let the mirrors be a distance L apart and the clock move at speed v. In the moving frame the light goes straight up: one tick is τ = 2L/c. In the lab the light goes along the hypotenuse of a triangle of half-width v Δt/2, so 

(c Δt / 2)² = L² + (v Δt / 2)²
Δt = 2L / (c √(1 − β²)) = γ τ,    γ = 1 / √(1 − β²)

That is time dilation, derived from nothing but "light goes at c for everyone". The Minkowski diagram at the bottom shows the same statement geometrically: the vertical line is the lab worldline, the tilted one is the rocket, the yellow diagonals are the light cone, and the dashed hyperbola c²t² − x² = c²τ² is the set of events one proper second after the origin. Both clocks reach that hyperbola after one tick of their own — they just reach it at different heights on the lab's time axis.

Nerd note

This is not a metaphor for anything. Muons made 15 km up in the atmosphere live 2.2 µs and should decay in the first few hundred metres; at γ ≈ 20 they reach the ground, and detectors count them every day. Caesium clocks flown around the world in 1971 came back tens of nanoseconds off, as predicted. GPS satellites run 7 µs/day slow from their speed and 45 µs/day fast from weaker gravity; the +38 µs/day net correction is compiled into the satellites, and without it your position would drift by about 10 km a day.
