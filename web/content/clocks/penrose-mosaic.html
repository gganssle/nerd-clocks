How to read it

Three hands, none of them a hand. The hour is the rotation of the whole mosaic — the gold arrow on the rim marks where noon has turned to. The minute is the zoom: the tiling swells continuously by a factor of φ = 1.618 034 over sixty seconds, and because inflation by φ maps the tiling onto itself, it snaps back to where it started at the top of every minute without a seam. The second is the light sweeping around, firing each rhombus as it passes. The digits in the middle are there for people in a hurry.

How it works

The tiling is a Penrose P3, built by repeatedly deflating Robinson triangles — half-rhombi with angles in multiples of 36°. Each step replaces a triangle with smaller copies of itself: 

thin  →  1 thin  + 1 fat
fat   →  2 fat   + 1 thin,   each φ times smaller

Seven rounds of that from a ten-triangle "sun" gives the mosaic you are looking at. The tiling is aperiodic: no translation maps it onto itself, so no patch of it ever repeats on a lattice — yet any finite patch you can find does recur infinitely often elsewhere. Count the rhombi and the ratio of fat to thin converges on φ, which is exactly why the tiling cannot be periodic: a periodic tiling would force that ratio to be rational.

Nerd note

Roger Penrose found this pair of tiles in 1974, cutting the known aperiodic set down from Berger's original 20 426 tiles to two. In 1984 Dan Shechtman saw a tenfold diffraction pattern in an aluminium–manganese alloy — forbidden for any crystal — and was told for years that it could not exist. It was a three-dimensional Penrose tiling. He got the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. In 2023 the hunt finished at one tile: the "hat" einstein, a single shape that tiles the plane only aperiodically.
