How to read it

Every tick is a toss. A ball of laser-cooled caesium leaves the trap at the bottom exactly on the second, flies up through the microwave cavity, turns over at the apex half a second later, and falls back through the same cavity — one complete parabola per second. The counter across the top runs from 0 to 9 192 631 770: that number of cycles of the caesium hyperfine transition is the second.

How it works

Six beams of 852 nm light (invisible to you — this is infrared) cool a cloud of caesium to about a microkelvin, then a slight frequency offset between the upward and downward beams throws the ball into the tube. On the way up it gets a microwave pulse; on the way down, half a second later, it gets a second pulse. That is Ramsey's method of separated oscillatory fields, and the interference between the two pulses produces the fringe pattern on the right: 

P(δ) = cos²(π δ T),   T = flight time between pulses ≈ 1 s

The central fringe is about 1 Hz wide out of 9.2 GHz, a line quality factor near 1010. The servo never sits on the peak — it probes alternately at the two half-height points (the gold dot hopping side to side) and steers the oscillator until the two answers match. Atoms that made the transition come back in F = 4 and fluoresce brighter on the way down.

Nerd note

Since 1967 the second has been defined as 9 192 631 770 periods of the radiation from the transition between the two hyperfine levels of the ground state of caesium-133 — a definition chosen to match the astronomical second that came before it, then frozen. The 2019 SI revision went further: the metre, the kilogram, the ampere and the kelvin are all now pinned to fixed constants, and every one of them is measured through a clock. Getting the second right is how you get the rest of the SI right. A fountain like NIST-F2 is good to about one part in 1016; raise it by 3 cm and general relativity shifts its rate enough to notice.
