How to read it

The disc is the whole sky as you would see it lying on your back: the zenith is in the centre, the horizon is the rim, north is at the top and east is on the left (it has to be, because you are looking up, not down at a map). Stars, planets, the Sun and the Moon are drawn where they really are for this location.

Two rings turn with the sky. The white meridian pointer marks the line due south (due north in the southern hemisphere): read your local time on the orange ring and local sidereal time on the blue ring. The small dot on the orange ring marks today's local solar noon, when the Sun crosses the meridian.

How it works

The Earth turns once relative to the stars every 23 h 56 m 4 s, a sidereal day. Our clocks follow the Sun, and because Earth also moves about 1° along its orbit each day, it needs about 4 extra minutes of spin to bring the Sun back to the meridian. That is the 24-hour solar day.

Sidereal time is simply the right ascension currently crossing your meridian. It is computed from Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time plus your longitude:

GMST = 280.46062° + 360.98564737° × (JD − 2451545.0) LST = GMST + longitude

Each star's altitude and azimuth then follow from its right ascension (α) and declination (δ), your latitude (φ) and the hour angle H = LST − α. The map uses an azimuthal-equidistant projection: distance from the centre is proportional to 90° − altitude, so the altitude circles are evenly spaced.

The Sun and Moon come from low-precision almanac series, and the planets from JPL's Keplerian orbital elements (accurate to a fraction of a degree). The sky colour follows the Sun's altitude through civil (−6°), nautical (−12°) and astronomical (−18°) twilight.

Things to notice

  • Each star rises about 4 minutes earlier every night, which adds up to 2 hours a month. That is why the winter and summer constellations differ.
  • Polaris barely moves. Its altitude equals your latitude, and the two end stars of the Big Dipper's bowl point straight at it.
  • The planets and the Moon always stay near the dashed ecliptic, the plane of the solar system.
  • The Moon's lit side always faces the Sun, even when the Sun is below the horizon.

Nerd note

Star positions are for epoch J2000. Precession of the equinoxes has since shifted them by about a third of a degree, too little to see at this scale. In about 12,000 years Vega, not Polaris, will be the pole star.
