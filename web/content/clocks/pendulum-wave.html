How to read it

The big numbers are hours and minutes. The seconds are the pattern: at :00 every bob hangs in a single straight line. Watch the line twist into a travelling snake, split into two and three interleaved rows, dissolve into apparent chaos around :30, and then reassemble itself just in time for the next minute.

How it works

A pendulum's period depends only on its length (for small swings), not on the mass of the bob:

T = 2π √(L / g)

The longest pendulum here makes exactly 51 swings per minute, the next 52, and so on up to 68. Solving for the length gives each one a precise size: from about 34.4 cm down to 19.3 cm.

Because every pendulum completes a whole number of swings in 60 seconds, all of them are back where they started at the end of each minute. In between, neighbouring pendulums drift out of phase by 1/60 of a cycle per second, which is what draws the waves.

Why the patterns appear

  • At t = 60/k seconds, every k-th pendulum lines up again, so you see k interleaved snakes. At 30 s you get two rows swinging in opposition, at 20 s three, at 15 s four.
  • This is the same effect as aliasing in digital sampling and moiré patterns: you are sampling a smooth phase gradient at 18 discrete points.
  • The bobs are completely uncoupled; no pendulum influences another. All the apparent choreography is pure arithmetic.

Nerd note

Real pendulum-wave demos slowly fall out of step because the simple formula ignores air drag and the fact that the period grows with swing amplitude (by about 1% at 23°). This one is computed from the wall clock, so it never drifts.
