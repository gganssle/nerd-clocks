How to read it

Each of the 86 400 seconds in the day is one dot. The spiral starts at midnight in the middle and winds outward; the marker rides the current second, and everything behind it is already burned — so the lit region is the fraction of the day that has gone. Blue dots are prime seconds, grey dots are composite, and the marker flares white-gold whenever the second count itself is prime. The panel on the left factorises the current second and tells you how long until the next prime one.

How it works

This is a Sacks spiral, an Archimedean spiral with one full turn per perfect square: 

r = √n     θ = 2π√n

Because θ advances by exactly 2π between consecutive squares, every square lands on the ray pointing straight up, and quadratic polynomials in general fall on smooth curves rather than scattering. That is what makes prime-rich quadratics visible to the eye — the orange curve is n² + n + 41, Euler's 1772 polynomial, which is prime for every n from 0 to 39 and still produces primes at an absurd rate afterwards. Ulam's 1963 square spiral shows the same diagonals; Sacks' 1994 version just makes the quadratics round.

Nerd note

There are 8 403 prime seconds in a day, and the prime counting function π(n) tracks them as the day goes. The crude form of the prime number theorem, n / ln n, guesses 7 601 — nearly 10% low. The logarithmic integral ∫₂ⁿ dt/ln t guesses 8 440, off by less than half a percent, which is why li(n) is the estimate number theorists actually use. Twin primes sit back to back on the spiral; the longest prime-free stretch of the day is 72 seconds. And Euler's polynomial is not luck: it is prime for 0…39 exactly because the field ℚ(√−163) has class number 1, which is also why eπ√163 misses being a whole number by less than 10−12.
