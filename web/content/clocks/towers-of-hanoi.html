How to read it

At midnight all 16 disks sit on peg A. By the next midnight they have all moved to peg C, one disk at a time, never putting a bigger disk on a smaller one. The optimal solution takes 65,535 moves, so one move happens about every 1.318 seconds, and the move number tells you how far through the day you are.

The biggest disk moves exactly once, halfway through the solution, so it lands on peg C at 12:00:00 noon. The second-biggest moves at 6:00 and 18:00, and so on down.

How it works

The recursive solution moves n disks from A to C like this: move n−1 disks out of the way to B, move the biggest disk to C, then move the n−1 disks from B onto it. That gives

moves(n) = 2·moves(n−1) + 1 = 2ⁿ − 1

and 2ⁿ − 1 is also the minimum: the biggest disk has to move at least once, and each time it does, every other disk has to be stacked on one other peg.

The binary connection

  • On move m, the disk that moves is given by the number of trailing zero bits of m. Disk 1 moves on every odd move, disk 2 on every move divisible by 2 but not 4, and so on.
  • Each disk always travels in the same direction around the three pegs, so its position is just (moves so far × direction) mod 3. This clock computes the whole tower directly from the move number, with no replay.
  • The Gray code row (k XOR k≫1) changes exactly one bit per move, and bit i flips precisely when disk i moves. The highlighted bit is the disk in motion.

Nerd note

Édouard Lucas published the puzzle in 1883 with a legend: priests in a temple move 64 golden disks, and when they finish, the world ends. At one move per second, 2⁶⁴ − 1 moves take about 585 billion years, roughly 42 times the current age of the universe.
