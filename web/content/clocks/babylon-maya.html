How to read it

Left, the clay tablet: hours, minutes and seconds written in Babylonian cuneiform. Each column is one base-60 digit built from two symbols: a vertical wedge worth 1 and a corner wedge worth 10. Three corner wedges and four verticals make 34.

Right, the stela: today's date as a Maya Long Count, one base-20 numeral per row. In each numeral a dot is 1, a bar is 5, and a shell is zero. Under it are the two cycles of the Calendar Round, the 260-day Tzolk'in and the 365-day Haab', and at the bottom the time of day in twentieths of a day.

Why base 60?

Sumerian and Babylonian scribes counted in sixties by about 2000 BCE. Sixty divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, so fractions like 1/3 come out clean. Babylonian astronomers passed the system to Greek astronomers, who passed it on to us. That is why an hour still has 60 minutes, a minute 60 seconds, and a circle 360 degrees.

Babylonian notation had no zero and no "decimal point". The same wedges could mean 1, 60 or 1/60, and a missing place was just a gap that is easy to misread. Late scribes in the Seleucid era (c. 300 BCE) added a placeholder of two slanted wedges for an empty middle place, used here for 00. But they never treated it as a number in its own right.

The Maya Long Count

The Maya had a true zero centuries before it reached Europe. The Long Count tallies days since a mythological creation date, 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u (11 August 3114 BCE), using mostly base 20:

1 k'in = 1 day 1 winal = 20 k'in = 20 days 1 tun = 18 winal = 360 days ← the one base-18 step 1 k'atun = 20 tun = 7,200 days 1 b'ak'tun = 20 k'atun = 144,000 days

Converting from our calendar uses the GMT correlation: Julian Day Number 584,283 equals Long Count 0.0.0.0.0. The Tzolk'in pairs a number 1–13 with one of 20 day names, and the Haab' has 18 months of 20 days plus 5 unlucky Wayeb' days. Together they repeat every 52 years.

Nerd note

On 21 December 2012 the count rolled over from 12.19.19.17.19 to 13.0.0.0.0, the end of the 13th b'ak'tun, which set off a wave of doomsday hype. Classic Maya inscriptions never predicted an ending; one monument at Palenque projects dates thousands of years beyond 2012. The world carried on, and so does this counter.
